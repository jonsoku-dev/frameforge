# Develop the picture before the timeline

A readable poster that changes its heading is not yet a directed film. Before filling the timeline, make the picture convincing at the intended delivery size. This is a production step performed by the maker, not a request for the user to approve every sketch.

## Reference and direction

Use an accessible reference relevant to the brief. Record the exact page or artifact and what was actually observed: a still supports composition and asset observations; it does not support claims about pacing or animation. Identify the transferable choice, not a branded asset to copy. For example, a reward promotion may use a large expressive character and a physically connected reward reveal; that does not require its character, palette or layout.

In `prompt.md`, name the visual thesis and its likely failure: “a courier earns the cape through flight” / “a mascot floating above reward cards would hide the action.” Choose the media for that thesis: authored illustration, licensed photography, local 3D, typography or procedural rendering. No medium, shape count or effect is inherently professional. Record rights, actual available tools, and substitutions. Do not assume an image service or model is available or authorized.

When the whole result is rejected, compare the actual candidate and reference at a useful display size before choosing the next edit. Name the dominant visible gap: for example, identical flat edge treatment across fur, cloth and metal; an unbounded background effect where the reference has an authored focal object; or a diagrammatic world with no foreground/midground separation. A count of micro-fixes does not answer that rejection. Choose a shot-level correction that addresses the gap, and preserve the prior output for comparison. A change of medium, more detail or a longer prompt is not itself evidence of improvement.

## Build one representative shot

Implement the actual hero, type, light and composition of a demanding shot before duplicating layouts across the film. Use the authorized still or render path and inspect its full-size output and a version at the intended small display size. If the renderer's preflight effects are not authorized, use another permitted observation or mark the result unverified. Do not treat a source-code description as the image. A raw asset render may diagnose geometry or light, but inspect its intended type, copy, framing and environment together before calling the representative shot ready. Then test a short consequential sequence before spending on the full timeline.

Look for specific defects:

| Decision | Evidence to inspect | Revision when weak |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Character or object | Silhouette, expression/pose, meaningful details, material and contact with its world | Redraw/repose/reframe the hero; do not merely add particles |
| Composition | First focal point, foreground/background separation, scale and negative space | Change shot size, overlap or camera position; not uniform enlargement |
| Typography | Family and weight suited to the voice, deliberate line breaks, spacing, contrast at display size | Recompose the lockup with actual licensed fonts; preserve required copy |
| World and finish | Consistent perspective, light, edge treatment, texture scale and palette roles | Resolve inconsistent asset styles or flat spatial relationships |

Write the observed defect and corrective decision, then inspect the revised shot. This is a qualitative judgment with evidence, not an aesthetic score or a user acceptance claim. For a deliberately flat/minimal film, judge the craft of proportion, alignment and timing; do not add shading or detail simply to satisfy this list.

## Prove the motion idea

Extend that shot through its most consequential action and inspect before, during and after it. A courier approaching three stars, making contact and wearing the earned cape expresses a different idea from a stationary character beside “collect three.” A music title changing scale and spatial role at a phrase change expresses more than the same title card with another word. These examples explain causality, not reusable shot templates.

For a consequential physical action, make an event record before revising it: the approach, preparation, contact/release frame, reaction, recovery and persistent result. State the coordinates' owner (world, camera, character or prop), what holds the object at each phase and when its ownership changes. At a parent/coordinate-space handoff, preserve the world position, rotation, scale and appearance (including inherited strokes or font metrics) before starting the next path; inspect adjacent saved frames on both sides of the handoff. This is a way to debug causality, not a demand that every film use a five-pose cycle. A star that follows the hero until it vanishes can look like an overlay; an independent approach that meets a palm, travels with the closed hand and enters a bag makes the collection observable. A planted foot should retain its world contact while the body compresses and recovers; moving the whole character up and down alone does not establish weight.

Inspect a saved sequence spanning those phases, including the frames immediately before and after contact. Check gaze, body inclination, articulation, silhouette and delayed follow-through at the intended small size. Keep the destination and its opening visible through the important handoff: a mathematically correct path into a pouch hidden behind the face or arm can read as eating instead. Fix the staging and draw order, not just the path or an added effect. Verify that counters, reward states and sound cues describe the action actually completed, rather than announcing success before the prop arrives. Preserve requested audio; if cue timing changes, record and validate that change. Compare the prior and revised output at corresponding event phases, not only the most attractive still. Report any continuity jump or unobserved phase rather than inferring the action from code or a burst effect.

Plan anticipation, action, arrival and reading time for the subject; choose cuts, curves, holds and camera motion for their purpose. Repeated layouts, uniformly spaced changes and generic ease-in/scale entrances are evidence to question, not automatic failures. A useful hold should remain a hold even if a motion diagnostic flags it.

Then complete the film. Compare saved MP4s at corresponding events; inspect the finish as well as information accuracy. Fix the largest remaining evidenced weakness, not a fixed count of cosmetic issues. If the user rejects the overall direction or craft, record that rejection and revisit concept/assets/shot design. Do not reinterpret it as a request for larger numbers. Technical PASS, self-review and user acceptance remain separate statuses.
